## THE DISPATCH

In July, Compass Pathways released the complete six-month dataset from its second Phase 3 trial of synthetic psilocybin (COMP360) for treatment-resistant depression. It's the most detailed durability picture this compound has produced to date.¹ On the 25 mg dose, 39 percent of participants had a clinically meaningful response at week 6 and maintained it, on average, through week 26. Among week 6 responders who had not yet reached remission, 28 percent got there after retreatment during the trial's blinded 26-week follow-up.¹ The clinical question this newsletter has tracked since spring, whether the effect lasts, now has a provisional answer. The new question is narrower and more practical: what happens, procedurally and financially, when a patient needs retreatment outside a research protocol?

## THE SCIENCE

Compass Pathways' second Phase 3 trial, COMP006, enrolled 581 participants with treatment-resistant depression. On average, participants were in a current depressive episode lasting more than three years and had made more than six prior treatment attempts over their lifetime.¹ They were randomized to 25 mg (296 participants), 10 mg (142), or 1 mg (143) of synthetic psilocybin, given as two fixed doses three weeks apart. The 10 mg and 1 mg arms served as active comparators, not an inert placebo.² At the week 6 primary endpoint, depression severity on the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) fell 3.8 points more in the 25 mg arm than in the 1 mg arm, a statistically significant difference (p&lt;0.001).² That between-arm comparison, rather than the response rate within the 25 mg arm alone, is the trial's estimate of the drug's specific contribution, although functional unblinding remains a known methodological challenge in psychedelic trials, even with active low-dose comparators.

The July data extends that picture to week 26. Thirty-nine percent of the 25 mg arm, 116 of 296 participants, had a clinically meaningful response at week 6 and maintained that benefit, on average, through week 26.¹ "Clinically meaningful" has a specific definition here: a reduction of at least 25 percent in MADRS score. That's a lower bar than the 50 percent reduction commonly used to define "response" in depression trials, and it matters when comparing this figure with other studies.

The retreatment finding is the part worth sitting with longest. Among the 68 participants in the 25 mg arm who had a clinically meaningful response at week 6 but had not yet reached remission, 28 percent went on to reach remission after an additional dose during Part B, the trial's blinded follow-up period.³ Remission here means a MADRS score of 12 or below, with no single item scored 4 or higher. Compass has not published how many of those 68 participants actually took the additional dose, and the company describes this as a post hoc analysis, so the figure is best read as a provisional signal until the full dataset appears in a peer-reviewed paper. Even so, it's the practical core of this release: the first Phase 3 signal that a course of COMP360 may not be a one-time treatment event, but potentially the start of a maintenance model, with further doses given when response fades rather than on a fixed schedule. Safety through 26 weeks stayed consistent with the earlier data. Serious adverse events occurred in 5.7 percent of the 25 mg arm and 6.3 percent of the 1 mg arm, with no new safety signal at the higher dose.¹

This is where the clinical finding runs into an infrastructure problem this newsletter has flagged for months, with little movement to report. A psilocybin-assisted session is not a pill. It requires a supervised six-to-eight-hour administration with trained clinical staff present throughout, a delivery model with no equivalent in standard psychiatric billing. The billing infrastructure insurers use to process claims is only partly in place. The American Medical Association's Category III Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for monitoring during psychedelic medication therapy (0820T to 0822T) took effect in January 2024, but Category III codes are temporary tracking codes without the established payment values that routine reimbursement depends on.⁸ Diagnoses are already coded under ICD-10, and drug-specific HCPCS codes typically follow FDA approval. Compass's rolling FDA submission remains on track for the fourth quarter of 2026, and a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher (CNPV) could compress the review that follows to as little as one to two months.⁴ But FDA approval answers only one question: whether the drug can be prescribed. It doesn't answer whether, or how, a clinician gets paid for the supervised hours the treatment requires, or how a patient who needs retreatment months after a first course gets it covered. That billing gap is still open.

The esketamine precedent, discussed in this newsletter previously, showed that a novel psychiatric therapy can move from FDA approval to meaningful insurance coverage within one to two years when the delivery model is relatively contained: a supervised session plus two hours of monitoring. Psilocybin's supervised window runs three to four times longer. The retreatment data doesn't change the substance of that gap. If anything, a therapy that may need to be given again when response fades makes solving it more urgent, since the billing question would then come up for the same patient more than once.

## THE STORY

The clinical picture from this July's data release is specific. A two-dose course of synthetic psilocybin was followed by a clinically meaningful antidepressant response (at least a 25 percent drop in MADRS score) in close to four in ten patients on the 25 mg dose, and that response held, on average, through six months. For context, these were patients who had, on average, made more than six previous treatment attempts. Among early responders who had not yet reached remission, 28 percent got there after retreatment.³ It's a meaningful, if still provisional, durability signal from a Phase 3 trial with 581 participants.

The gap sitting next to that data is just as concrete, and it belongs to no single actor. Only temporary Category III tracking codes exist for supervised psychedelic-assisted therapy, so no insurer, public or private, yet has an established, valued way to pay for the session hours this treatment requires, let alone a retreatment session.⁸ No single company, regulator, or insurer created this gap. It predates this data, and this data doesn't close it on its own.

Here's what is moving. Compass's rolling NDA submission continues toward its targeted fourth-quarter 2026 filing, and the CNPV program remains in place to compress the review that follows.⁴ Billing infrastructure typically follows regulatory approval rather than coming before it, which is why the gap tends to widen right after the news gets most exciting. Watch CMS and the relevant professional coding bodies over the next two quarters for any move toward permanent Category I codes. That signal, more than the approval news itself, will decide how quickly this durability data becomes something a patient can actually use more than once.

## YOU ASKED

**Q: "If a patient of mine responds well to psilocybin-assisted therapy once it's approved, and the effect fades after six months, what actually happens next under current billing?"**

Today, very little is structured. Temporary Category III CPT codes for monitoring during psychedelic medication therapy have existed since January 2024, but they are tracking codes without established payment values, so once the drug itself is FDA approved, there will not yet be a routine way for a clinician to be reimbursed for a first course, let alone a retreatment.⁸ In practice, a clinic offering retreatment would have to negotiate payment directly with the patient or a self-insured employer plan until coding catches up. Access looked similar in the early years after esketamine's 2019 approval, though psilocybin's longer supervised session makes the coding problem more complex. The retreatment data in this issue is a provisional answer to a clinical question. Billing is a separate, unresolved question, and patients deserve to hear honestly that the two don't move on the same timeline.

---

**This week's takeaway:** Compass's six-month data points to a durable antidepressant response following a two-dose course of synthetic psilocybin, and to a meaningful chance of remission after retreatment. Both findings are still provisional until peer review. The billing infrastructure needed to pay for that treatment, or for a retreatment, doesn't exist yet, and nothing in this data changes that timeline.

**A question worth sitting with:** If a therapy's own trial data suggests patients may need it more than once, does building the reimbursement infrastructure before approval become a clinical necessity rather than an administrative convenience?
